How this was made

A concept site for a product that does not exist, built around one painting of light finding a boat in a storm. Here is the craft behind it.

The idea

The starting point was a single looping video: a shaft of light breaking through storm clouds onto one small boat. It already told a story about being found in the dark, so the product became a pocket distress beacon, and the light became the brand. The gold in every button is sampled from that beam, and the warm black from the clouds around it.

Everything after that tries to keep the beam present: it follows you down the page as a thin gold line, lights the sea in every header, and appears again as the strobe on the 3D beacon.

A sea drawn in code

The sub-page headers first used enlarged crops of the painting, and they looked blurry. They were replaced with a storm sea drawn on a canvas at the screen's real pixel density: rows of swell lines packed toward the horizon, a pool of gold where they cross the beam, and rain that glows inside the light.

The first version drew a fresh gradient on every line, every frame, and ran at about 5 frames a second in testing. Batching everything into about 13 strokes, redrawing at 30 fps and pausing off screen made it cost almost nothing, with the same look.

A beacon you can press

With no product photography, the beacon is a real-time three.js model built from simple shapes, with a printed label drawn onto a canvas texture. You can lift its cap, press the button, watch the amber light find a fix, and see the strobe and radio waves start while the stage goes dark. Four hotspots pinned to the model explain each part, and plain buttons do the same for keyboard and screen reader users.

three.js loads only when the model is about to be seen, after the page has loaded, so the rest of the page is never kept waiting.

The night on a chart

The rescue page tells one fictional night in a sideways timeline. An SVG chart above it follows along: the fall, the press, the signal to the satellite, the drift every five minutes and the lifeboat's route. A switch shows the same night without a beacon, where the search starts seven hours late, and a replay plays the night on its own.

Sound without files

Sound is off by default. Turned on, a storm fades in: surf made from filtered noise that swells slowly, and a soft hiss of rain. The beacon adds a blip on each strobe flash and a chime when the alert goes out. All of it is synthesised in the browser with Web Audio, so there is nothing to download.

Making it fast

Atmosphere is expensive, so every effect was measured. Lighthouse scores for a simulated slow phone, before and after the performance work:

PageBeforeAfterMain content shown
Home77965.3 s to 2.4 s
Rescue75945.6 s to 2.6 s
FAQ48945.4 s to 2.5 s
The beacon51845.6 s to 2.7 s

The biggest wins: the hero video went from 11.4 MB to 1.1 MB, the painting from 633 KB to 52 KB as WebP, headlines stopped starting invisible, and the 3D scene stopped building before anyone could see it.

For everyone

Every page scores 100 for accessibility in Lighthouse. With reduced motion turned on, the intro, smooth scrolling and flashing are replaced by still frames and steady light. Everything interactive works with a keyboard, announces its state to screen readers, and keeps text at readable contrast, including the dim words that light up as you scroll.

Tools and process

  • Built withHTML, CSS and JavaScript with no build step. GSAP and ScrollTrigger for scroll animation, three.js for the beacon, Canvas 2D for the sea, SVG for the chart, Web Audio for sound.
  • MediaFFmpeg for the video encodes and WebP images, Chrome screenshots for the share card.
  • Checked withLighthouse, the Impeccable design detector and critique, and a headless Chrome harness that scrolls each page and records frame times.
  • ProcessDesigned and built by Naser Demiraj with Claude Code, using design skills for structure, visual direction, motion and review. Every change went through a reviewed pull request.
